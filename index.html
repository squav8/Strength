Smaller daily shifts are easier on your body clock. Shifts of 15–30 minutes a day are hard to notice; most people can move their clock about an hour earlier, or one to two hours later, per day.

Why it works this way

  • Keep your times regular. Matthew Walker's first rule in Why We Sleep is to go to bed and wake at the same times every day. The plan only moves your times when a flight forces it, and moves them in small daily steps.
  • Prepare before an early flight. Moving your bedtime earlier in steps over the days before an early departure lets you keep more of your sleep on the night that matters. Bright light soon after waking helps your clock move earlier; dim light in the evening helps too.
  • You can't go to sleep much earlier on demand. In the hour or two before your usual bedtime your body is at its most alert, so the plan won't schedule bedtime more than about an hour earlier than your body is used to. If an early flight still cuts the night short, it says so and suggests a nap or plane sleep.
  • Short naps, early in the day. A nap of 20–30 minutes before about 3 pm can top you up without hurting the next night. Late or long naps reduce your sleep pressure at bedtime.
  • Caffeine lasts. Caffeine's half-life is roughly 5–6 hours, so an afternoon coffee is still partly active at bedtime. Each day shows a caffeine cut-off about 8 hours before bed.
  • Alcohol fragments sleep. It helps you nod off but breaks up the second half of the night and suppresses REM sleep. Skip the airport and in-flight drinks if you want the night to count.
  • Short trips can stay on home time. For stays under about three days, staying on home time often disrupts you less than adapting and then adapting back.

This planner gives general guidance, not medical advice. If you have a sleep disorder or take medication that affects sleep, check with your doctor.
